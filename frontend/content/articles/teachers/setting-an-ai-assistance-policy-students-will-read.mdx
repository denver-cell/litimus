## Three lines, not three pages

State what is allowed, what is not, and what disclosure looks like for anything in between. That is the whole policy. A longer document invites students to skim past it, and skimmed policies produce the “I didn’t know that counted” conversations that make a flagged score harder to act on.

## Put it on the assignment, not just the syllabus

A policy buried in a syllabus from week one is functionally invisible by week ten. Repeat the relevant line on the assignment sheet itself, next to the due date.

## Give one example of each category

Allowed: brainstorming topics. Not allowed: generating paragraphs to edit lightly. Disclose: using a tool to reword a rough draft for clarity. Students argue over categories less once they have seen a concrete example of each.

## Say what happens with a borderline score

Tell students in advance that a mixed score is not a finding on its own, and what your process is if one comes up — see <a href="/for/teachers">the full five-step process</a> for what that looks like. Knowing the process exists before an assignment is due changes how a flagged result lands.
