<Callout>

**Before the meeting.** Read the report yourself, compare it against a baseline of the student’s earlier work, and decide what you are actually asking them — not “did you cheat” but “walk me through how you wrote this.”

</Callout>

## Open with the process, not the score

Tell the student what you looked at and why: the assignment, their policy, and a report that flagged some passages. Naming the process first signals that this is routine, not a verdict already reached.

## Ask, don’t accuse

“Can you walk me through how you wrote this?” works better than “this looks AI-written.” The first invites an explanation. The second invites a denial, and you learn nothing either way.

Useful follow-ups:

- Where did the structure come from — an outline, a template you were given, a first draft?
- Which parts took the longest, and why?
- Can you show me an earlier version, in Google Docs or Word history?

## What not to say

- “The detector says X%” as if the number were the finding. It is one input.
- Anything that assumes guilt before the student has answered.
- A threat of consequence before you have actually established what happened.

## If the explanation holds up

Say so, plainly, and move on. A student who was treated fairly on a false flag remembers it — and English-language learners and writers of formulaic genres are exactly the group most likely to need that fairness, per the research on detector bias cited below.

## If it doesn’t

Follow your institution’s academic integrity process from here — a report is evidence to bring into that process, not a substitute for it.
