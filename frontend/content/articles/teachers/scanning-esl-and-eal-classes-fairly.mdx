## Know the research before you scan the first essay

Independent research has found that widely used AI detectors consistently misclassify writing by non-native English speakers, in some studies flagging a majority of honest essays from learners while barely flagging native-speaker writing at all. That is not a Litimus-specific problem, but it is not one to assume away either.

## Set the baseline from this class, not a general one

A learner’s earlier work in your own class — not a generic “average student” expectation — is the fair comparison point. Sentence variety and vocabulary range grow over a course; a score that looks high in week two can look ordinary by week ten against the same student’s own trajectory.

## Weight the conversation more than the number

For this population specifically, treat the score as a prompt to look closer, not a threshold to act on. The five-step process still applies — read the work yourself first, then compare against baseline, then talk to the student — but the gap between “number” and “decision” should be wider here than for a class where false positives are rarer.

## What to tell the class up front

Say plainly, before any assignment is due, that detectors are known to score non-native writing differently, and that this is factored into how you read results. Knowing that in advance changes how a flagged score lands for a student who was already anxious about it.
