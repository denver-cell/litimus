## Say what you actually do, not a general pledge

“All writing is human-written” is a claim you likely cannot fully guarantee, given how research and editing tools are normally used. A more accurate, more defensible line names your actual process: what you use tools for, what you don’t, and that you can share drafts or a report on request.

## One line, in a place people already look

Your about page or author bio, not a separate policy document nobody will find. The goal is the same one-paragraph-not-three-pages approach that works for any policy: short enough that someone actually reads it before asking.

## Update it if your process changes

A disclosure statement is a snapshot of how you work right now, not a permanent pledge. If your use of any tool changes, the statement should change with it rather than quietly go stale.

## This protects you as much as it reassures a reader

A standing, dated statement is evidence of a consistent position if your work is ever questioned — better than reconstructing what you would have said, after the fact, about a specific piece.
